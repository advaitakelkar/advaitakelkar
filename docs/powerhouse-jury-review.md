# The Powerhouse — a jury's questions

Questions an architecture jury would put to the thesis as it stands on 1 November 2026
(Chapters 1–4, the hypothesis and the framework). Written to be answered one by one; each
section lists the argument as the thesis makes it, then the questions. Hard ones first.

Answer under each question. Where an answer changes the text, note which section moves.

---

## The hypothesis

*"An Electric Vehicle Powerhouse complex will turn charging activities into an opportunity
for enhanced civic and community engagement. If the extra time leads only to more retail, the
hypothesis fails."*

1. **Is this a hypothesis or a design intention?** A hypothesis can be false. What observation,
   on an existing site or on your design, would make you say "the Powerhouse does not work"?
   Name the number and the threshold, not the method.
2. **"Enhanced" relative to what?** Compared with a gas station, a parking lot, a plaza, a
   library? If the baseline is a forecourt, almost anything is an enhancement. Pick the
   baseline that makes the claim hard.
3. **The retail failure clause cuts both ways.** A food hall, a café, a grocery, a market and a
   laundromat are retail. Where exactly is the line between "programme" and "more retail", and
   who draws it: you, the operator, or the people counted on site?
4. **Does the charger do any work in the argument?** Chapter 3 says the building should work
   with no one charging; Chapter 2 says faster charging empties the pause. If the programme is
   what makes the place, why is this a thesis about charging rather than about public buildings
   next to parking?
5. **Civic engagement is claimed; civic engagement is not measured.** Minutes on site, share of
   site open to all and programme count measure presence and provision. None of them measures
   engagement: people meeting, organising, returning, trusting. What would?

## Chapter 1 — Introduction

### 1.0 The pause

6. The opening says each transport mode "built a place for its pause" and the longer the pause
   the more public the building. The gas station is the counter-example you then cite: a long
   history, a short pause, nothing public. Is the rule "longer pause, more public" a historical
   finding or a wish? Give a second counter-example and explain it.
7. Automation is mentioned and dropped. A driverless taxi charges with no person in it. If
   fleets go driverless, who is the Powerhouse for?

### 1.1 A new road system

8. The energy argument (engine efficiency, 28 per cent of emissions, 120 million EVs by 2040)
   justifies electrification, not the Powerhouse. What in 1.1 is evidence for a building?
9. Four in five charges happen at home, and most vehicles "should charge where they already
   spend time" (2.2). You are designing for the fifth charge. How large is that fifth, in
   people and in minutes, in the settings you care about?
10. You cite a review finding that what persuades people to switch to EVs "is far less settled
    than usually assumed". If public charging may not drive adoption, the Powerhouse cannot
    claim to accelerate the transition. Does it claim to?

### 1.2 The pause today

11. Penn Station and the airport are the precedents for the civic pause. Both are forced
    waits of fixed length at a fixed threshold, inside a building the traveller cannot leave.
    A charge stop is none of those things: the driver can walk away. Why is the station the
    right model and not, say, the laundromat or the playground, where people also wait?
12. The GRIDSERVE forecourt and the Tesla diner are called "commercial answers" to a "public"
    demand. On what evidence is the demand public rather than commercial? Aktuna's study finds
    ambivalence toward the spectacle, not a wish for a civic room.
13. Truck parking is the industry's second most pressing problem. That is an argument for
    parking, which drivers have asked for, not for programme, which they have not. What have
    drivers asked for?

### 1.3 What is a charge stop?

14. The three stays (20 minutes, 1–2 hours, a night) organise the whole thesis. 2.4 then shows
    the real stop depends on energy added and average power, and 2.6 that it is shortening. Are
    the three stays a finding about charging or a design decision you have chosen to keep?
15. A gas station "treats every visitor the same". So does a library. Why does variety of stay
    length require variety of programme rather than one good room?

### 1.4 Programme makes the place

16. Kayden found two in five bonus plazas marginal. Those plazas had no charger either. What in
    the Powerhouse stops it becoming a marginal plaza with a charger in it?
17. "Chosen for each site, from what people nearby lack." Who decides what a neighbourhood
    lacks, by what survey, and what happens when the lack is housing, which the catalogue does
    not contain?
18. "Part of every site is open to anyone, with nothing to buy." Who pays for it, who cleans it,
    who keeps it open at 2 a.m., and what happens when the operator stops?

### 1.5 The network around the charger

19. The rings place programme by stay length. But a clinic visit (1–2 h) and a drivers' room
    (a night) are not longer versions of a coffee; they have different users, hours, security
    and entrances. Is "distance from the charger" really the organising dimension, or is it
    access (3.5)? If the latter, the rings diagram is wrong.
20. Stan Allen's field: the charger "fixes the services and leaves the uses free to change".
    A charger fixes the vehicle, not the person. What does the charger actually fix for the
    programme around it that a bus stop or a parking lot would not?
21. The michi-no-eki have "one standard core of rest and toilets". Is the Powerhouse's standard
    core the charger or the toilet? Be honest: which one would you remove last?

### 1.6 The proposition

22. The method is comparative: existing gas stations, service areas, truck stops and hubs read
    on three measures. None of these comparisons has been done yet. What has the thesis found
    so far, as opposed to argued?
23. Malls and supermarkets are set aside "because there the shopping draws the charger". A town
    Powerhouse with a market and a grocery is a mall-shaped argument. Why is it in and the
    supermarket out?
24. "Faster charging does not undo the argument... it depends on a programme worth staying
    for." Then the title is wrong. Why is the building named for the one component you say it
    does not depend on?

### 1.7 Three ways of reading

25. Sustainability, phenomenology and social theory each "lead" a chapter. Which finding in
    Chapter 2 would be different without McHarg? Which in Chapter 3 without Heidegger? If none,
    the lenses are decoration.
26. Foucault is named for the risk of a watched place, Jacobs for the answer. A charging site
    is cameras, licence-plate readers, app log-ins and payment records by design. How does a
    place whose core transaction is surveillance become Jacobs's street?

## Chapter 2 — The conditions of the pause

### 2.0 The network

27. 290,019 ports, 33 cars per public point, 2.7× growth. These numbers describe scarcity of
    plugs. Scarcity argues for more plugs, cheaply and fast: the opposite of a building. Why is
    the right response to a plug shortage a civic complex?
28. "Half the fast ports belong to Tesla." The Powerhouse is public by access. Can it be built on
    a proprietary network at all, and if not, who are the realistic clients?

### 2.1 The ground of the road

29. Parking covers 14 per cent of LA County; cars stand still 95 per cent of the time. The
    conclusion drawn is "the ground already exists". But the ground that exists is privately
    owned, zoned for parking and produces rent. How does a Powerhouse get it?
30. Two routes that never cross: vehicles to chargers, people to rooms. Every precedent in 2.3
    that worked (caravanserai, inn, concourse) put the person through the vehicle's space or
    above it. What is lost by separating them, and is "never cross" even possible on a tight
    city block?

### 2.2 Where and how we charge

31. The 2030 scenario needs 25.7 million home ports and 182,000 public fast ports. Public fast
    charging is under one per cent of the ports the country needs. Is the Powerhouse a type for
    the whole transition or a niche for the apartment dweller and the long-distance driver?
    Say which, and size the type accordingly.
32. "A long connection does not have to mean a long wait beside the car." Agreed; then the
    Level 2 driver (4–10 hours) is at work or at home, not at the Powerhouse. Which charger
    level does the Powerhouse actually serve, and does that one give you 20 minutes or 1–2 h?

### 2.3 An architecture of waiting

33. The three moves (protected interior, legible threshold, room sized to the pause) are read
    from eight cases spanning a thousand years. What is the test that these three, and not
    others (for example a reason to come back, or a view of the vehicle), are the ones that
    matter? Where in the cases is the counter-evidence?
34. You note the comparison "cannot settle cause and effect": good places may hold people
    longer rather than long pauses making good places. Then neither can your design, which
    will have both. How will the site study separate them?
35. The electric forecourt (GRIDSERVE) "brings back a room for the person, but the plan is
    still the forecourt's". Draw the alternative plan now, in one line: where is the bay
    relative to the room in a Powerhouse, and why is that better than beside it?

### 2.4 Vehicles, time and demand

36. Table 2.1 says its rows "cannot be ranked against one another". Then what does it show
    that the sentence "stops vary from minutes to hours" does not?
37. "Each Powerhouse will still need its own study of catchment, vehicle schedules and grid
    connection." These are the numbers that size the building. Without them, how do you know
    the type is one building and not a parking structure, or a kiosk?
38. The two clocks (vehicle plugged in, person on site) are the thesis's best instrument. Where
    have they been run so far, on how many people, at which site?

### 2.5 The rules of the stop

39. NEVI requires four 150 kW ports open 24/7. A thirty-minute truck break and a ten-hour rest
    are set by law. These rules are your strongest argument for a building that is never
    empty. Why does the thesis treat them as "conditions" rather than as the brief?
40. "No civic station category exists yet." Which approvals (occupancy, fire, zoning, overnight
    accommodation) would a mixed Powerhouse need, and has any jurisdiction ever granted that
    combination to one building?

### 2.6 The literal Powerhouse

41. Megawatt charging and battery swapping shorten the stop to minutes. You answer that the
    programme then "carries more of the reason to be there". At that point the Powerhouse is a
    community centre with a substation. Is that the thesis? If so, say it in Chapter 1.
42. Battery buffering cuts grid capacity by 50–80 per cent and runs through a power cut. This
    is the one claim that makes the building literally a powerhouse and a civic resource
    (shelter, resilience). It is one paragraph. Why is it not the centre of the argument?
43. Three layers at three speeds (energy, charging, public). Who owns each layer, and what
    stops the long-lived public rooms being sold or closed when the short-lived charging layer
    is no longer profitable?

## Chapter 3 — The place of the pause

### 3.0 A new kind of station

44. Bertolini's node–place model was built for rail stations, where the node is a fixed timetable
    and thousands of people a day. A charging site is a strong node only in the energy and
    road network, not in a passenger network. Is a site with twelve bays a "node" in Bertolini's
    sense at all, and does the model survive the transfer?
45. "Almost every charging site built today is an unbalanced node." Unbalanced nodes in Bertolini
    become balanced because the node brings footfall that place-making can capture. A charging
    site brings cars whose occupants are in them. Where is the footfall?
46. Jewel Changi drew 40–50 million visitors, 60 per cent of them residents. It cost about
    S$1.7 billion. What is the scale of Powerhouse this precedent licenses, and what is the
    scale you can build?

### 3.1 What people need

47. Klinenberg's social infrastructure saved lives in the heat wave because neighbours already
    knew each other through streets, shops and organisations. A Powerhouse is new, at the edge,
    on a road. Does placing social infrastructure beside a highway make it, or break it?
48. Carr's five needs become "provisions": toilets, seats, shade, a view, rooms, a changing
    programme. These are what any decent park provides. What does a Powerhouse provide that a
    park with a charger on its edge does not?
49. Loneliness (one in two adults) is cited as the need. What evidence links the kind of
    co-presence a charging stop produces (strangers passing, twenty minutes) to reduced
    loneliness, as opposed to the weak-tie theory it gestures at?

### 3.2 A place without the plug

50. The no-plug test: 17 of 22 programmes work with no one charging. This is the strongest and
    most dangerous result in the thesis. If the building works without the plug, the plug is
    optional. State plainly what the charger adds that the test does not capture: money,
    footfall, energy, a reason for the public sector to fund it. Then defend that.
51. The Yangtze Delta study and michi-no-eki both show non-travellers making the place. Both
    are nodes in passenger systems with public funding and local government as partner. Who is
    the host municipality for a Powerhouse in the United States?
52. Michi-no-eki are 1,230 stations across a country with national designation, a ministry and
    a 30-year programme. Is the thesis proposing a building or a programme? If a programme, who
    runs it; if a building, why cite the programme?

### 3.3 An icon for the transition

53. Acceptance research says people resist "a technical object dropped into a landscape". The
    Powerhouse is a large technical object with a building around it, dropped into a
    landscape. What makes it belong rather than arrive?
54. CopenHill is an icon because a ski slope is absurd on a power plant. What is the
    Powerhouse's absurdity, the thing people would come to see? "Visible structure and a solar
    roof" describes every warehouse built since 2015.
55. Sklair's critique (icons serve private interest) is taken as the brief. Then the brief is
    public ownership. Where in the thesis is the public owner?
56. Does the type need to be an icon at all? A town michi-no-eki is not. Is the icon argument
    for the city setting only, and should 3.3 say so?

### 3.4 From dark lot to green ground

57. Asphalt at 152°F is the Arizona number. Is heat the problem in the settings and climates you
    will design in, or is it rain, snow, salt and wind? The ground argument should follow the
    site's climate, not the strongest statistic.
58. Trees over charging bays, planted ground under vehicles, lawns beside bays: trucks need
    turning radii, clear height and hardstanding; chargers need cable runs, drainage and
    bollards. How much of the "green ground" survives a truck?
59. WHO links green space with health. The dose matters. How much green ground, in square
    metres per visitor, is the Powerhouse promising, and is it more than the tree pit at the
    kerb?

### 3.5 Open, with rooms that close

60. Easterling: the rules built into infrastructure decide who may enter. The Powerhouse's
    rules are an app, a payment system and an operator with a lease. What are the written rules
    of the open layer — can someone sleep on the bench, sell on the plaza, charge a phone,
    stay all day — and who enforces them?
61. Three layers in plan: open at the centre, set-hours around it, operated at the edge with
    their own doors. The set-hours ring then walls the open centre from the street at night.
    Is the open ground actually reachable at 2 a.m., or only in the diagram?
62. "Open and safe to run." Safe for whom? Show the night plan from the point of view of a
    woman alone, a teenager, and a truck driver asleep.

### 3.6 What a Powerhouse can hold

63. The catalogue was sorted with an AI model's help and the author's review. What was the
    sorting rule for "20 min" versus "1–2 h", and how many entries would a different reviewer
    move?
64. 22 programmes, three settings, three access layers: a Powerhouse could be anything from a
    toilet block with a canopy to a hospital with parking. What is the smallest Powerhouse
    that still deserves the name, and what is the largest before it stops being one?
65. The table has no column for who pays, who operates, and what it costs to run at 2 a.m. The
    framework in Chapter 4 inherits the omission. Add the column, or explain why the thesis is
    allowed to leave it out.

## Chapter 4 — A framework for the pause

### 4.0 Five steps

66. Read, recognise, arrange, plan the day, measure. Step 5 measures the building after it is
    built. Where is the step that tests the framework before design: the comparison promised
    in 1.6?
67. The framework "does not fix a site or a form". A framework that fixes nothing cannot be
    wrong. Give one site the framework rejects and one it would accept, and say why.

### 4.1 Reading the site

68. Node reading: people without home charging, EVs per port, freight routes, road access,
    grid. Place reading: five needs and Moreno's six functions. These are two lists, not a
    method. What is the scale, the unit, the data source and the threshold for each, such that
    two readers would place the same site in the same position?
69. Chorus and Bertolini's five positions each get a response. "Unbalanced place: bring the
    charger in, at the place's scale." Why would a lively neighbourhood with no charging want
    a charger? Whose problem is being solved?
70. "A dependent site is not a Powerhouse site at all." Dependent sites are where people cannot
    charge at home and have the fewest third places. The framework excludes the people the
    social argument is about. Reconcile this.

### 4.2 Three settings

71. Corridor, city, town. The city form is "compact and stacked, charging below, public rooms
    above": a mixed-use building with a garage, the most common building in any downtown. What
    does calling it a Powerhouse change?
72. The town form is a michi-no-eki. The corridor form is a service plaza with rooms. The
    precedents already exist and work. Is the contribution the type, or the argument that the
    three are one type? If the latter, what does a corridor site learn from a town site?
73. Bremen's mobil.punkt is on-street car sharing; Crossrail Place is a station roof garden
    over retail. Neither has a charger at its centre. Why are they precedents for the city
    Powerhouse and not for the kind of thing the thesis set aside in 1.6?

### 4.3 Arranging the Powerhouse

74. Six rules: rings, three moves, two routes, access layers, lifespan layers, green ground.
    Rules 1 (rings by stay) and 4 (layers by access) sort the same rooms by different keys.
    Which wins when they disagree: a drivers' room is 'a night' (outer ring) and 'operator'
    (edge), but a cooling shelter is 'a night' and 'open' (centre)?
75. "Two routes that never cross" and "a court people and vehicles share" (2.3) are both rules.
    Draw the plan in which both are true.
76. The rules "give the type its consistency". Which rule, if broken, means the building is not
    a Powerhouse? If none is necessary, the type has no definition.

### 4.4 Across the day and night

77. Table 4.2 populates every six hours with a user group. Who is on site at 3 a.m. on a town
    site with no freight corridor? The table is written for the mixed site that makes the
    argument easiest.
78. Jacobs's safety comes from a street with many eyes, each with a stake. A truck driver
    asleep behind a curtain and a night-shift worker drinking tea are two eyes, both tired. Is
    that the night plan, or is it lighting and cameras after all?

### 4.5 Measuring the pause

79. Minutes on site: a longer person-clock "counts only if comfort, access and a free programme
    explain it" (2.4). How will you know what explains it? That is an attribution problem the
    timed count cannot solve.
80. Share of site open to all: a parking lot is 100 per cent open to all with nothing to buy.
    The measure rewards emptiness. Add the condition that makes it meaningful.
81. Programme on site: counts programmes, not use. A clinic with no patients scores the same as
    one with a queue. Why count provision rather than occupancy?
82. The comparison with gas stations, service areas, truck stops and hubs "uses the same three
    measures". When? Name the sites, the dates and the hours of observation planned before
    Thesis 01 begins. Without them the measures are untested.

### 4.6 From framework to building

83. Seven principles: node and place in balance, charger at centre, public court, energy roof,
    green ground, layers at their own speeds, public icon. Five of the seven would describe a
    good market hall with a solar roof. Which two are specific to charging, and are they
    enough to carry a thesis?
84. "The thesis argues that the type can be repeated; the design shows what one looks like."
    One design cannot show that a type repeats. What in Thesis 01–02 will test repeatability:
    a second site, a second setting, or a kit of parts?

## Three questions the jury will end on

85. **What is the one thing a Powerhouse does that nothing else does?** Not a list. One
    sentence that a gas-station operator, a transit agency and a town council would each
    recognise as new.
86. **Who is the client?** A utility, a charging network, a municipality, a developer, a fleet?
    Each gives a different building. The thesis has not chosen, and the framework cannot be
    tested until it does.
87. **What would make you abandon the type?** Give the finding from the site study that would
    send Thesis 02 back to a parking structure with a good café.
